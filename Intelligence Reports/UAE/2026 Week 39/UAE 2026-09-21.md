## Story 1: Oil prices ease as Saudi Arabia claws back exports, but Hormuz itself stays near-shut

**Source:** Gulf News | https://gulfnews.com/business/energy/oil-prices-ease-as-markets-weigh-saudi-supply-recovery-against-hormuz-risks-1.500681414
**Related vault notes:** [[Comparison Network]]

Brent crude eased to $103.00/barrel (down 0.89%) and WTI to $99.22 (down 1.08%) as of 10am Tokyo time today, with Murban — the Gulf-specific benchmark that matters more for UAE producers and Asian buyers — down 2.86% to $117.90. The pullback is being read by traders as exactly that: a pullback, not a resolution. Saudi Arabia has restored exports above 4 million barrels per day in September, up sharply from 2.4 million bpd in August, by rerouting around pipeline damage and running offshore ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman. That is the direct cause of today's price relief.

It sits alongside a much grimmer supply picture. Only four commodity vessels transited the Strait of Hormuz on 17 September, against a normal daily flow of roughly 85 — a number essentially unchanged from the "Hormuz effectively closed" state that has held for weeks. The IEA has now pushed back its forecast for a full recovery in Gulf production to 2027 and expects global oil supply to fall by 5.7 million bpd in 2026; the EIA separately expects Middle East flows to stay constrained through Q4, with roughly the same volume shut in. Reuters reporting from Sunday and Monday's trading session also noted that a UAE Sharjah–Oman land corridor, opened in May to route consumer goods, food and medical supplies around the closure, has seen the value of goods carried jump 66% in its first three months to $463.21m, with over 34,000 truck movements — a tactical workaround for non-energy trade, not a substitute for the 20 million barrels a day and one-fifth of global LNG that normally passes through Hormuz.

For Orwell, this is background rather than action: none of the comparison network sites price directly off Brent, but the wider point — that a "price easing" headline today sits on top of a supply picture the IEA doesn't expect to normalise before 2027 — is the correct way to read every future "oil calms down" story out of the Gulf for the next year. Today's move is a Saudi logistics win, not evidence the underlying crisis is receding.

## Story 2: Trump's Gulf diplomacy widens to Iraq and Jordan; UAE's Gargash warns table to prepare for "unstable Iran"

**Source:** The National | https://www.thenationalnews.com/news/mena/2026/09/21/iraq-and-jordan-to-join-trump-gcc-meeting-as-us-arab-diplomacy-intensifies/

During UN General Assembly high-level week in New York, President Trump's meeting with GCC officials was expanded today to bring in Iraq and Jordan, with Egypt also expected to join. UAE diplomatic adviser Dr Anwar Gargash represented Abu Dhabi (UAE Minister of State Abdullah bin Zayed was separately confirmed attending a related multilateral session hosted by the US President). Three items dominated the agenda: Iran and its regional proxies, the Houthis' expanding control of Yemen's western coast and the Bab Al Mandeb strait, and the 30 September withdrawal of the US-led anti-ISIS coalition from Iraq.

Gargash's framing is the line worth remembering: he called for a "new mindset" on Iran, arguing that regional stability talks need to happen directly between Iran and Arab states rather than being run purely at US-Iran level, and he told the room the UAE has to prepare for an "unstable" Iran in the near term. That is a notably more pessimistic public posture than the UAE has generally taken through this crisis — it reads as Abu Dhabi hedging against a longer, messier endgame rather than betting on a near-term deal, and it lines up with the oil market's own assumption (Story 1) that this doesn't resolve quickly.

Bab Al Mandeb is the angle to watch alongside Hormuz: it's the second chokepoint, and today's meeting put Houthi control of it on the same agenda as Iran itself. If that thread escalates, expect it to hit the Red Sea shipping/insurance conversation the way Hormuz has hit oil — worth a line in any future InsureCompare content on marine war-risk cover, though there's nothing actionable there today.

This is also the live update to yesterday's parked "Iran-US conditions for talks" open loop: rather than repeating a restated Iranian position, today's movement is the diplomatic track itself widening to more parties, with the UAE taking a more hawkish public tone than before.

## Story 3: US Senate weighs diesel export ban as global fuel crisis deepens — UAE prices show the gap

**Source:** The National | https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/energy/2026/09/21/why-a-us-diesel-export-ban-would-only-make-the-global-fuel-crisis-worse/

US Senate Majority Leader John Thune said today he was "open to exploring" a ban on US diesel exports in response to domestic prices that have gone parabolic — California pump prices above $10/gallon, a national average near $6.30/gallon, which the piece calculates as equivalent to crude at $265/barrel despite crude itself trading near $100. The US currently exports about 1.8 million barrels a day of diesel and similar distillates plus 440,000 bpd of jet fuel; a ban would remove a chunk of global supply just as the market is already short.

The root causes read straight back into this week's other two stories: the effective closure of Hormuz is the primary driver, compounded by Ukrainian strikes knocking out roughly 1 million bpd of Russian diesel exports and Houthi attacks on Saudi pipelines and refineries that normally supply Europe and East Africa. There's also a composition problem — the medium-gravity Middle Eastern crude that refineries want for diesel is being replaced by lighter US grades that yield less of it, so even barrel-for-barrel replacement doesn't fully fix the shortage.

The UAE angle is the contrast, not a threat: diesel here sits at Dh4.30/litre (about $4.43/gallon), a fraction of the US price, because domestic refining and subsidy structures insulate the local pump. The piece's own argument is that a US export ban would be counterproductive — it would spike prices for import-dependent neighbours like Mexico and Canada, invite retaliatory restrictions, and damage US credibility as an energy supplier, echoing 1970s oil-shock dynamics. None of that touches UAE motorists directly today, but it's a live illustration of how far the Hormuz closure's second-order effects have travelled, and worth having on file if diesel costs start showing up in any Orwell-adjacent logistics or shipping conversation.
