## Story 1: DHS bypassed a judge to pull 10,000 calls and texts from a journalist facing federal charges over an ICE protest

**Source:** The Dissenter | https://thedissenter.org/defying-court-dhs-seized-logs-of-10-000-calls-and-texts-from-journalist/
**Author:** Kevin Gosztola | **Published:** 30 August 2026 | **Read time:** ~5 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Journalist Georgia Fort, charged alongside Don Lemon and Shane Bollman over their coverage of an ICE protest at Cities Church in St Paul, Minnesota (arrests directed by Attorney General Pam Bondi), discovered through her defence lawyers that DHS had issued a customs summons to T-Mobile for six months of her call and text logs, roughly 10,000 records, without notifying her, after a magistrate judge had already declined to authorise a warrant for the same material. Prosecutors had described "the core offense" as occurring over approximately two hours; Fort's lawyers argue the six-month sweep bears no relation to that timeframe. The government separately collected more than 40,000 pages and hours of video surveillance; Google refused a parallel subpoena for her YouTube channel records. Fort's team has asked the court to impound or destroy the seized logs.

The legal mechanism is the story: DHS used a customs authority meant for inspecting imported goods to reach a journalist's communications after being refused through the normal warrant process. Former DHS lawyer Chris Duncan is quoted calling it "an improper use of the subpoena," since "this is not a customs case." Magistrate Judge John F. Docherty flagged that the government's application omitted the Privacy Protection Act, the same omission The Guardian's Sam Levine reported in an FBI warrant targeting Washington Post reporter Hannah Natanson, suggesting a pattern rather than a one-off drafting error. On 12 August the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press had already urged dismissal, arguing journalists cannot be prosecuted under the Freedom of Access to Clinic Entrances Act for asking questions of protesters. ACLU attorney Nathan Freed Wessler's point cuts to the accountability gap directly: "Without knowing how many of these subpoenas there are and what they're being used for, there's no way for courts or lawmakers or the public to put checks on executive branch abuses."

The evidentiary core is court filings (the defence motions, the magistrate's prior ruling, the Reporters Committee's dismissal brief) plus on-record legal commentary from a former DHS lawyer and the ACLU, solid and independently checkable. The claim that the customs-summons routing mirrors the Natanson case rests on Gosztola citing The Guardian's reporting rather than an independent comparison of the two warrant applications; treat that specific parallel as reported-elsewhere rather than verified here.

## Story 2: The US struck Iranian positions for the first time since July's ceasefire, and the sanctions that came with it look mostly cosmetic

**Source:** Responsible Statecraft | https://responsiblestatecraft.org/larak-island-iran-strikes/
**Author:** Trita Parsi | **Published:** 30 August 2026 | **Read time:** ~4 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

The Trump administration struck Iranian positions on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz, the first acknowledged US military action against Iran since Trump announced a ceasefire in July, framed as pre-empting Iranian mine-laying in the strait. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent paired the strike with sanctions on Banque Misr, Egypt's second-largest bank; Parsi's analysis argues the sanctioned institution is minor relative to the finance channels actually moving Iran-linked money, reading the move as more symbolic than substantive. Parsi's own assessment is that Trump is not pursuing a diplomatic resolution or full escalation, but managing a low-level conflict through the midterms, keeping Iran off the front pages while avoiding the domestic political cost of visibly higher oil and petrol prices. He argues new sanctions combined with increased traffic through the strait's southern corridor have shifted the pressure balance toward Iran, though he flags three ways that could unwind: Trump overextending into a strike serious enough to draw retaliation, current measures proving too weak to change Tehran's calculus, or Iran timing a delayed response for maximum midterm disruption. Regional diplomats are described as still engaged in active back-channel talks, against Trump's public refusal to negotiate.

This is Quincy Institute analysis built on the administration's own announcements (the strike, the Bessent sanctions) rather than original document-based reporting; the strategic reading, that the administration is managing rather than resolving the conflict, is Parsi's own interpretation of the pattern, clearly presented as such rather than as an internal admission.
