## Story 1: ICE coordinated Iranian deportation flights with Tehran's own government while the US and Iran were at war

**Source:** The Intercept | https://theintercept.com/2026/08/18/iran-trump-deportation-flights-ice/
**Author:** Noah Hurowitz | **Published:** 18 August 2026 | **Read time:** ~7 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Per the granularity rule this is a same-day catch-up: it carries an 18 August date and had not yet been reported.

The Intercept reports that Immigration and Customs Enforcement coordinated directly with the Iranian government to deport more than a hundred Iranian nationals to Tehran during and after the June 2025 "Twelve-Day War" between Israel, the US and Iran, reversing decades of practice that had shielded Iranians from removal because of the persecution risk on return. The evidentiary core is hundreds of internal ICE emails spanning May to November 2025, obtained via a Freedom of Information Act request by the National Iranian American Council and given to The Intercept exclusively.

The emails show ICE Acting Director Todd Lyons directing staff to "launch target cases as a priority" and removal-operations head Marcos Charles ordering a plan to "remove all removable FO Iranians," coordinated in part through Qatari intermediaries and Iranian government representatives. On one flight, an ICE attaché in Tel Aviv noted that Iran's own embassy had requested three specific individuals be added to the manifest and that a wrong person was accidentally boarded, writing only that "this is unfortunate." Four charter flights are documented, the first carrying 54 of an expected 120 deportees between 29 September and 1 October 2025; one deportee attempted suicide before being physically forced onto a plane, per contemporaneous New York Times reporting cited in the piece. NIAC president Jamal Abdi is quoted saying the emails newly establish that "it was actually in the middle of the [Twelve-Day] war where the White House was directing ICE to start removing Iranians." DHS told The Intercept the allegations were "FALSE" and that standard consular protocols were followed; the department did not address the specific emails.

The evidentiary core, the internal emails documenting direct coordination with Tehran and White House pressure to prioritise Iranian removals mid-war, rests on primary government documents obtained through FOIA, about as strong a basis as this register sees. What remains less settled is DHS's blanket denial, which characterises the story as false without engaging the specific documents quoted, leaving the two accounts in direct and unresolved conflict.

## Story 2: A six-month investigation traces the funded network that campaigned against a Cambridge academic before his death

**Source:** Byline Times | https://bylinetimes.com/2026/08/18/the-peter-thiel-linked-race-science-network-that-penetrated-cambridge-university-and-targeted-jason-arday/
**Author:** Nafeez Ahmed | **Published:** 18 August 2026 | **Read time:** ~9 min

Per the granularity rule this is a same-day catch-up: it carries an 18 August date and had not yet been reported.

Byline Times, following a six-month investigation involving interviews with nearly 20 current and former Cambridge staff and students, maps a network it says drove the media campaign that preceded the 14 August death of Cambridge academic Jason Arday. The campaign began on 21 July when academic Nathan Cofnas published plagiarism allegations against Arday, framing him as a "DEI hire" without merit; Liverpool John Moores University had already investigated similar claims and found "citation defects" it attributed to honest error and inadequate supervision related to Arday's autism, not plagiarism. The allegations generated over 200 articles in major British media.

The piece traces funding and personnel links from Cofnas through Cambridge associate professor James Orr, who is also Reform UK's head of policy and a Free Speech Union advisory council member, to Silicon Valley financier Peter Thiel, who made a "five-figure donation" to Cambridge channelled through Orr. It documents a wider infrastructure: Aporia magazine and the Human Diversity Foundation, Quillette (edited by Claire Lehmann), the Free Speech Union (founded by Toby Young), and a Westminster hub called "The Sanctuary" funded by cryptocurrency figure Ben Delo, who separately donated £4 million to Reform UK. Byline Times traces the intellectual lineage to the Pioneer Fund, a 1937 body with historical eugenics ties, which it says "relaunched" under the Aporia brand in 2022. It documents a coordinated publication timeline in the run-up to Arday's death, including a Quillette essay calling him a "fantasist, fabulist, fraud" published on 14 August, before his death was publicly announced. Cofnas, Orr, Lehmann and the Free Speech Union all deny coordinating with one another on the coverage; Cambridge called the volume of allegations "unsubstantiated" and said it prevented "legitimate media enquiry."

The evidentiary core, that Thiel funded Cambridge activity via Orr and that Delo funded the Westminster hub housing Aporia, rests on documented donations, Companies House records and named-source interviews, a solid documentary basis. The claim of a *coordinated* campaign against Arday specifically is circumstantial: a timeline of related publications from network-adjacent figures, denied on the record by every named party, rather than direct evidence of coordination. Treat the funding-network mapping as well-evidenced and the coordination claim as the weaker, contested part of the story.

## Story 3: Analysts warn Western coverage of Ukraine's "breakthroughs" is funded by the same arms industry it fails to hold to account

**Source:** Responsible Statecraft | https://responsiblestatecraft.org/is-ukraine-winning-the-war/
**Author:** Branko Marcetic | **Published:** 18 August 2026 | **Read time:** ~6 min

Per the granularity rule this is a same-day catch-up: it carries an 18 August date and had not yet been reported.

Responsible Statecraft argues that a recurring media pattern, declaring a Ukrainian military "breakthrough" that fails to materialise, has repeatedly undercut chances for a negotiated end to the war. Marcetic walks through four cited episodes: the Kerch bridge strike, the 2023 counteroffensive (which the piece says gained 143 square miles against 331 square miles of Russian advance, at a cost of tens of thousands of Ukrainian casualties), the 2024 Kursk incursion (which it says ended in Ukrainian expulsion and helped scuttle ceasefire talks), and a recent 40-day drone campaign inside Russia that yielded little territorial change despite being framed as tide-turning. The piece names the Center for European Policy Analysis and the Atlantic Council as sources of the optimistic assessments driving this pattern, describing both as funded by weapons manufacturers, and argues Russia's subsequent escalations against Ukrainian ports and energy infrastructure, threatening a 1.5 percent GDP hit, have followed each hyped episode.

This is analysis rather than a documents-based investigation: no new funding disclosure is presented for CEPA or the Atlantic Council beyond an assertion in the text, and the piece's central claim, that the breakthrough narrative pattern actively worsens Ukraine's negotiating position, is the author's interpretation of a contested war rather than a verified causal finding. It fits this register's remit on the strength of the funded-think-tank framing, a standing accountability question about who funds the institutions shaping war coverage, more than on original reporting.
