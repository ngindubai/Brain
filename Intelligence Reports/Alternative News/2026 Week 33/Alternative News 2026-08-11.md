## Story 1: The Pentagon's Indo-Pacific build-out is turning Hawai'i into a nuclear frontline and an environmental liability

**Source:** The Intercept (adapted from TomDispatch) | https://theintercept.com/2026/08/10/china-war-hawaii-military-cost-nuclear/
**Author:** William D. Hartung | **Published:** 10 August 2026, 6:00am ET | **Read time:** ~7 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Per the granularity rule this is a catch-up: it carries yesterday's date and had not yet been picked up when the 10 August report was written.

Hartung, drawing on a new Institute for Policy Studies report, argues the Pentagon's preparations for a potential war with China have made Hawai'i both a likely nuclear target and a site of long-term environmental damage that the military's own economic claims do not cover. He names Pentagon policy chief Elbridge Colby, grandson of former CIA director William Colby, as the architect of a shift toward shorter-range tactical nuclear weapons for a regional China conflict; Colby has said publicly he expects "World War III in the coming years" and that "we need to prioritize the potential for a conflict with China precisely in order to avoid it." A former Pentagon analyst, speaking to TomDispatch, is quoted calling this approach more likely, not less, to produce a nuclear war.

The IPS report's figures form the evidentiary spine: PFAS ("forever chemical") contamination of soil and groundwater at three Hawai'i installations, with remediation estimated near $500 million; the 2014 leak at the Navy's Red Hill Bulk Fuel Storage Facility, holding roughly 250 million gallons of jet fuel, which contaminated drinking water for 93,000 residents; a finding that the Pentagon has overstated its claimed $10 billion annual economic benefit to Hawai'i by around 30 percent; and a comparison showing military spending generates 5.3 jobs per $1 million invested against 12.3 jobs for equivalent spending on education, food production, healthcare or housing. The report also puts the federal government's outstanding back-rent liability to Hawai'i, separate from cleanup costs, at up to $133.7 billion, with two major military leases covering large tracts of Hawaiian land due to expire, which the report frames as a live opportunity for renegotiation.

Hartung sets this against a broader argument that China's global influence runs through trade and infrastructure, citing Belt and Road ties to 120 countries, rather than military assertiveness, noting China's last major conflict was the 1979 border war with Vietnam, in which by his account Chinese forces performed poorly. He proposes a return to the earlier US-China "common understanding" on Taiwan, limited US political recognition of independence in exchange for a Chinese pledge against forced reunification, as an alternative to continued build-up. University of Hawai'i researcher Jonathan Kay Kamakawiwoʻole Osorio is quoted calling the IPS report "a blueprint for reclaiming our nation here, in hope and faith in one another."

The evidentiary core, the contamination figures, the Red Hill leak scale, the economic-benefit and jobs-per-dollar comparisons, and the back-rent figure, is attributed throughout to the IPS report rather than independently re-derived by Hartung or The Intercept; the report itself is not linked in the fetched text, so its own sourcing (whose environmental testing, whose economic methodology) is not visible from this piece alone. The Colby "World War III" quote and the anonymous Pentagon-analyst quote are both drawn from a prior TomDispatch article rather than fresh reporting for this piece. The piece is best read as an analytical synthesis and advocacy argument built on a named think-tank report, not as an original documents investigation.

## Story 2: Pentagon drone, counter-drone and GPS-jamming tests are creating dangerous gaps in civilian aviation safety

**Source:** Responsible Statecraft | https://responsiblestatecraft.org/military-testing/
**Author:** Stavroula Pabst | **Published:** 10 August 2026 | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Per the granularity rule this is a catch-up: it carries yesterday's date and had not yet been picked up when the 10 August report was written.

Pabst reports that near-daily US military testing of drones, counter-drone systems and GPS jamming across American airspace is outrunning the interagency coordination needed to keep it safe for civilian aircraft, and that the failure has already contributed to a fatal crash. She cites a March 2025 Navy/Secret Service counter-drone test near Reagan Washington National Airport that generated false collision alerts for flight crews, and a 2020 MQ-9 Reaper drone crash at Syracuse's Hancock Field, which shares a runway with a commercial airport, causing over $6 million in damage that military officials withheld from public disclosure for more than a year. In February 2026 a Pentagon-lent counter-drone laser, deployed by Customs and Border Protection without notifying other agencies, struck party balloons its operators believed were cartel drones, prompting an unannounced FAA airspace closure that briefly raised fears of military action in Mexico; days later, military personnel destroyed an actual CBP drone with the same laser system, triggering a second unannounced closure around El Paso.

Senator Tammy Duckworth is quoted calling for an investigation into "potential serious and alarming problems" in interagency oversight, saying failures in planning create "an unacceptably dangerous situation." FAA Deputy Administrator Chris Rocheleau said the agency is being "nudged" to accept "more risk" around uncrewed drones. Dana Goward of the Resilient Navigation and Timing Foundation says he cannot be confident officials consistently know where these systems are operating or that adequate safeguards exist. The FAA and Pentagon reached a formal agreement in April stating the laser system's "safety controls work and do not pose undue risk to passenger aircraft," a conclusion the piece sets against the incidents it documents without directly disputing it.

On jamming specifically, Pabst identifies a May 2024 medical-flight crash in New Mexico as the first documented case of military GPS jamming testing directly causing a civilian aircraft accident: the plane lost GPS signal during testing at White Sands, and disoriented pilots flying by older methods crashed into a mountain; the NTSB investigation is ongoing. She cites 2021 IEEE Spectrum reporting on FAA internal records showing 233 GPS interference incidents in the southwestern US during 2017 and early 2018, a period of frequent testing, with five documented emergency declarations by pilots. An anonymous air traffic controller's report to NASA's Aviation Safety Reporting System states that White Sands jamming affected flight safety "to the point to where they couldn't navigate safely," and that the military often declines to pause testing when nearby pilots request it. Notices to Airmen covering thousands of square miles are the military's main warning mechanism, but Pabst reports they are too broad to tell pilots when or where interference will actually occur, leading many to disregard them. Todd Humphreys, a University of Texas aerospace engineering professor, is quoted acknowledging the testing "increases aviation risk in the short term" but arguing it is necessary to build anti-jamming technology that reduces risk over the longer term.

The evidentiary core rests on a mix of documented incidents (the Hancock Field crash and its concealed cost, the CBP laser incidents, the NTSB-investigated New Mexico crash), a specific FOIA-sourced dataset via a 2021 third-party investigation (IEEE Spectrum's review of FAA records), and on-record quotes from a sitting senator, an FAA official and two named subject experts. No Pentagon or FAA spokesperson is quoted directly responding to the specific safety criticisms in the piece as fetched, beyond the April interagency agreement's own conclusion; the "increases risk in the short term" concession comes from an academic rather than the military itself.
