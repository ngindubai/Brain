## Story 1: State Department recruits Palantir to advise on "free speech" and "countering digital surveillance"

**Source:** The Intercept | https://theintercept.com/2026/08/07/state-department-palantir-free-speech-surveillance/
**Author:** Spencer Norris | **Published:** 7 August 2026, 12:02pm ET | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Per the granularity rule this is a catch-up: it carries a 7 August date and was not caught by either the 8 or 9 August reports.

The Intercept reports that the State Department has enlisted Palantir Technologies, the data-analytics firm founded by Peter Thiel, as a partner in its new "Freedom Tech Excellence Program," under which Palantir employees will serve rotating assignments through 2029 advising the government on digital freedom, privacy-enhancing technology, counter-surveillance and "responsible AI governance," per the State Department's own published programme description. The piece sets that role against Palantir's existing government book: contracts with the military, the State Department itself and Immigration and Customs Enforcement, a data-analysis platform that, per Edward Snowden's leaked documents, underpinned NSA mass-surveillance programmes, and an ICE contract the piece says supported immigration raids and family separations. It also cites Palantir's access to identifiable patient data through New York City hospital contracts and to IRS databases, and notes the company sued the Swiss magazine Republik over its reporting on a failed contract bid, a case a Swiss court ruled against Palantir on.

Carrie DeCell of the Knight First Amendment Institute is quoted saying "neither this State Department, nor the partners they have announced so far, are trustworthy leaders in what they identify as online freedom of expression." David Greene of the Electronic Frontier Foundation argues that positioning a surveillance-industry firm as a free-speech adviser undermines the programme's own stated purpose and lowers the US's moral authority to make expression-rights arguments abroad. The piece also references a letter from ten members of Congress requesting information about what it characterises as Palantir's "mega-database" of government and citizen data.

The evidentiary core, Palantir's inclusion in the programme and the scope of its existing government contracts, rests on the State Department's own published programme materials and Palantir's own public contract record; the NSA-surveillance link is sourced to the Snowden leak rather than a fresh document, and the critical framing (that a surveillance contractor is unsuited to advise on digital freedom) is attributed to two named civil-liberties lawyers rather than asserted by the reporter. No on-record response from Palantir or the State Department to the specific criticism is included in the piece as fetched.
