## Story 1: Pentagon formally tells Congress it killed at least 150 Yemeni civilians, confirming yesterday's leak

**Source:** Responsible Statecraft | https://responsiblestatecraft.org/us-strikes-yemen/
**Author:** Stavroula Pabst | **Published:** 12 August 2026 | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Per the granularity rule this is a catch-up: it carries a 12 August date and had not yet been reported.

Responsible Statecraft reports that the Pentagon's annual civilian-casualties report, submitted to Congress, acknowledges for the first time that US military operations in Yemen killed civilians in 2025. CENTCOM found that three operations under Operation Rough Rider, targeting Houthi vessels attacking Red Sea shipping, "more likely than not" caused civilian harm: 153 people killed and 243 wounded, with strikes hitting a port, a residential building and a migrant detention centre in Saada. Washington had previously denied civilian casualties from the campaign and restricted strike details, citing operational security.

The figures land almost exactly where The Intercept's anonymous Pentagon sources placed them in yesterday's report on the same unreleased Section 1057 document, 153 killed and roughly 250 wounded, but this time as a formal submission to Congress rather than a leak to a reporter. Independent monitors still put the true toll higher: the Yemen Data Project counts 238 killed and 467 injured. Annie Shiel of the Center for Civilians in Conflict is quoted noting that "by its own count, the US killed over 100 civilians in boat strikes in 2025," and Emily Tripp of Airwars contrasts the caseload with the Iran campaign, where the US "chose to hit 13,000 targets in Iran in 40 days." The report separately claims zero civilian deaths from the Caribbean boat-strike campaign and omits any figure for the Iran conflict, where independent estimates put civilian deaths above 1,700 since February 2026.

The evidentiary core, the 153-killed, 243-wounded figure, now rests on the Pentagon's own document submitted to Congress rather than an anonymous leak, a meaningfully stronger footing than yesterday's account. The wider context, independent monitors' higher tolls and the omitted Iran and disputed Caribbean figures, is attributed to named sources at CIVIC and Airwars rather than presented as the Pentagon's own admission.

## Story 2: The Intercept sues Trump over paid early access to his own Truth Social posts

**Source:** The Intercept | https://theintercept.com/2026/08/12/trump-media-truthsocial-premium-first-amendment/
**Author:** Noah Hurowitz | **Published:** 12 August 2026, 10:48am ET | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Per the granularity rule this is a catch-up: it carries a 12 August date and had not yet been reported.

The Intercept and the Freedom of the Press Foundation filed suit against Donald Trump and his staff over Trump Media & Technology Group's "Truth API" programme, which charges up to $100,000 a month for early access to the president's Truth Social posts before the public sees them. The complaint argues the scheme violates the First Amendment by granting paid, preferential access to what amounts to official presidential communications, and separately invokes the Fifth Amendment against unreasonable fees for equal access to government information. The suit cites a Truth API press release in which Trump Media chief executive Kevin McGurn described the product as generating "high-margin, recurring revenue" for shareholders, alongside company filings showing $238 million in second-quarter losses and Trump's 52.1% stake in the company falling from an initial $4 billion valuation to roughly $1 billion.

Intercept editor-in-chief Ben Muessig is quoted: "Trump is trying to enrich himself by privatizing government information that he has no right to sell." Nikhel Sus of Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington adds that "every American is entitled to equal access to the president's public statements." The piece also references a Senate letter from Adam Schiff and Elizabeth Warren raising concerns that Trump has used Truth Social to hype individual stocks, a separate self-dealing question the lawsuit does not itself litigate but which sits alongside it.

The evidentiary core, the pricing and structure of the Truth API programme, rests on the company's own press release and public filings, both independently checkable; the constitutional claims are the plaintiffs' own legal theory, tested here for the first time in court rather than settled law. No response from Trump, his staff or Trump Media is recorded in the piece as fetched.

## Story 3: Farage referred to Met Police over undeclared £5 million crypto donation

**Source:** Byline Times | https://bylinetimes.com/2026/08/12/nigel-farage-reported-to-met-police-over-alleged-misconduct-in-public-office/
**Author:** Josiah Mortimer | **Published:** 12 August 2026 | **Read time:** ~5 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Per the granularity rule this is a catch-up: it carries a 12 August date and had not yet been reported.

Byline Times reports that Unlock Democracy and Compassion in Politics have formally reported Nigel Farage to the Metropolitan Police, alleging misconduct in public office over his failure to declare a £5 million donation from cryptocurrency billionaire Christopher Harborne, received shortly before Farage announced his 2024 parliamentary run. The complaint argues the non-disclosure breached parliamentary rules requiring MPs to register within one month any benefit that "might reasonably be thought by others to influence his or her actions, speeches or votes in Parliament." At the time of the gift Farage held no elected office but was already a Reform UK director, a GB News presenter and a prominent political commentator; Harborne's legal representatives maintain the gift carried no political conditions. The Met confirmed receipt of the report, saying only that it is "preparing a response and will revert in due course."

The story follows the same beat Byline Times has run for weeks, sits alongside separate, ongoing parliamentary scrutiny of Farage's undisclosed property holdings and donations from convicted fraudster George Cottrell, both referenced but not detailed in this piece.

The evidentiary core, that the £5 million donation was made and not declared within the required window, is not disputed by either side; the live question is whether the timing and Farage's public-facing roles at the time meet the legal threshold for misconduct in public office, a rare charge to secure against an elected politician. The complaint letter's wording is quoted directly; no independent legal assessment of the complaint's merits is included in the piece as fetched.

## Story 4: Dutch court hears case tying a whistleblower's murder to a $15-million-a-month bribery network

**Source:** OCCRP (with Follow the Money) | https://www.occrp.org/en/news/dutch-court-hears-case-against-whistleblowers-murder-suspect
**Author:** Esra Aygin (OCCRP) and David Davidson (Follow the Money) | **Published:** 12 August 2026 | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Per the granularity rule this is a catch-up: it carries a 12 August date and had not yet been reported.

OCCRP and Follow the Money report on a Dutch court hearing evidence against Hasan Y., a 31-year-old Turkish national accused of murdering Cemil Önal, a whistleblower shot dead on a hotel terrace in Rijswijk in April 2025. Önal was the former finance director for casino kingpin Halil Falyalı and had told Dutch prosecutors he was being tracked by hitmen after exposing an illegal gambling operation he said funnelled roughly $15 million a month in bribes to Turkish and Cypriot officials. The reporting details a specific law-enforcement failure: police detained Hasan Y. five days before the killing, in a vehicle with stolen licence plates, but released him before searching it thoroughly; the vehicle was later found to have carried a Glock pistol and ammunition.

Evidence presented in court includes surveillance footage placing Hasan Y. pacing near the hotel fifteen minutes before the shooting, and recovered firearms and clothing discarded near the scene. Hasan Y. was later arrested in Germany on a European arrest warrant; a second suspect, believed to be the actual shooter, is held in France on separate weapons charges. Bayram Bozkurt, a former Turkish prosecutor who was on the terrace at the time, has been provisionally released. No masterminds behind the killing have been publicly identified.

The evidentiary core, the surveillance footage, the recovered weapon and the pre-murder detention failure, is drawn from court proceedings and is independently verifiable through the case record; Önal's own bribery allegations remain his claims, not independently confirmed by the reporting, and are presented as the motive context rather than as established fact.

## Story 5: US Treasury permanently scraps beneficial-ownership reporting for domestic companies

**Source:** OCCRP | https://www.occrp.org/en/news/treasury-rolls-back-ownership-reporting-obligations-for-us-companies
**Author:** not bylined | **Published:** 12 August 2026 | **Read time:** ~4 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Per the granularity rule this is a catch-up: it carries a 12 August date and had not yet been reported.

OCCRP reports that the US Treasury has permanently eliminated beneficial-ownership reporting requirements for domestic companies and US citizens under the Corporate Transparency Act, the 2020 anti-money-laundering law that required disclosure of who actually owns and controls American businesses. Under the change, foreign companies must still report beneficial-ownership information to FinCEN where it concerns foreign individuals, but are now exempt from reporting on US citizens involved in their structures. FinCEN has said it will delete all beneficial-ownership data on US business owners already collected under the law.

The piece is short and carries no Treasury quote or named official defending the rollback, nor any on-record reaction from anti-money-laundering advocates; it reports the regulatory change and its mechanics rather than the political fight around it.

The evidentiary core, the substance of the rule change and the planned data deletion, rests on the Treasury and FinCEN's own public announcements and is not in dispute; what's missing from this account is any stated rationale from Treasury or assessment from transparency advocates of the change's likely effect, which is inferred by the outlet rather than sourced.
