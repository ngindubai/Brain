## Story 1: OCCRP names the alleged "enforcer" who shielded Cambodia's Prince Group from police

**Source:** OCCRP, co-published with The Straits Times | https://www.occrp.org/en/scoop/alleged-enforcer-in-prince-group-scam-network-identified
**Author:** Martin Young (OCCRP) and Andrew Wong (The Straits Times) | **Published:** 8 August 2026 | **Read time:** ~7 min

OCCRP identifies Chen Sokly as "Co-Conspirator-2" in the US Justice Department's October 2025 indictment of Prince Group chairman Chen Zhi, who faces wire fraud and money laundering charges over a Cambodia-based scam compound network. Prosecutors had anonymised Co-Conspirator-2 in the indictment; OCCRP and The Straits Times say the wording of the charging document, cross-referenced against corporate and property records, allowed them to identify him. The indictment quotes Co-Conspirator-2 boasting that "whenever there were law enforcement crackdowns at the scam compounds, nothing happened to 'us'," and alleges Prince Group earned over $30 million a day from fraud in 2018.

The reporting is built on a documents trail across four jurisdictions: Cambodian corporate records showing Chen Sokly chaired Awesome Global Investment Group from 2017 to 2022; a California registry showing three active companies and two property purchases from 2019, in Yorba Linda and San Marino; a Singapore property purchase and multiple directorships dated to mid-2017; and a Mauritius filing naming him director of Meritwise Group Public Ltd. The indictment alleges Co-Conspirator-2 monitored police investigations targeting Prince Group, directed a Chinese law enforcement official to have Cambodian police extort businesses on the group's behalf, bought a yacht worth over $3 million for a senior government official, and offered to "take care of" the son of a Chinese Ministry of Public Security official.

Financial links reported include a 50 percent shareholding in Sunseeker M Singapore Pte Ltd, a superyacht dealer where Sunseeker International's own spokesperson confirmed Chen Sokly worked as a sub-dealer until 2019 under the name "Martin Chen," and a shareholding in Oceanic Opportunity Fund PCC, which provided more than $50 million in loans to Prince Bank. OCCRP also reports shared directorships between Chen Sokly and three individuals linked to Huione Group, a separately sanctioned crypto firm, and that his wife, Li Mengmeng, bought a five-bedroom Los Angeles home in August 2020 through a seller, Fang Zhizhen, who is now herself sanctioned for Prince Group involvement and wanted in China since 2016 on hacking allegations; Li transferred the property into a trust two months after the October 2025 sanctions.

A Prince Group spokesperson rejected the allegations, saying the group would "challenge these accusations [and] expose the factual and chronological impossibilities" and calling the case "a coordinated international asset grab." The US Treasury Department declined to say why Chen Sokly himself has not been sanctioned. Chen Sokly has not been charged and did not respond to requests for comment; neither did Li Mengmeng.

The evidentiary core, the corporate, property and directorship trail across California, Singapore, Cambodia and Mauritius, rests on public and company registry filings rather than leaked material, and Sunseeker's own spokesperson corroborates the sub-dealer role independently of the indictment. The identification itself is OCCRP and The Straits Times's own inference from the indictment's wording matched against those records, not a claim the Justice Department has confirmed on the record; Prince Group's rejection and the Treasury's non-answer are both included rather than omitted.

## Story 2: Reform UK converted a £630,000 Tice loan into a donation as police investigate the party's funding

**Source:** Byline Times | https://bylinetimes.com/2026/08/07/reform-uk-accounts-raise-fresh-questions-about-origins-of-richard-tice-donations/
**Author:** Olly Haynes | **Published:** 7 August 2026 | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Per the granularity rule this is a catch-up: it carries yesterday's date and was flagged by today's search of Byline Times' homepage.

Byline Times reports that Reform UK began 2025 owing £883,000 on an interest-free loan from deputy leader Richard Tice's company, Tisun Investments. Filed accounts show the party repaid £270,000 of it and converted the remaining £630,000 into a donation. The piece places that conversion inside a wider pattern the Metropolitan Police is already investigating for possible breaches of electoral law: on 10 June 2024, Fiona Cottrell, mother of "convicted fraudster and Farage confidant" George Cottrell, transferred £1,075,000 to Tice's other firm, Britain Means Business Ltd; the same day, Britain Means Business donated £500,000 to Reform UK, and Reform UK repaid £200,000 of the Tice loan. Companies House filings cited in the piece show Britain Means Business held only £10,500 in capital by the end of 2024, leaving roughly £575,000 of Cottrell's transfer unaccounted for in the paper trail Byline Times could trace. A separate £1 million loan from Malcolm Robinson, of which £250,000 has been repaid, is flagged as a source whose identity "remains to be identified" in fuller detail.

The story sets these transactions against Electoral Commission donation data showing Reform UK drew 71.1 percent of its £31.8 million 2025 income from donations, £22.8 million in total, against 48.3 percent for the Conservatives and a £2.5 million deficit for Labour, and flags a separate anomaly: campaign spending of £4.37 million in 2025, a year with only local elections and a by-election, against £4.24 million in the 2024 general election year, alongside a £3.4 million fundraising spend against £1.4 million returned. Reform UK did not respond to questions about the campaign-spending breakdown or the fundraising gap; the piece includes no direct response from Tice. Green peer Natalie Bennett is quoted calling for a "radical overhaul" of the donation system, saying Reform has taken "millions from crypto billionaires and fossil fuel interests" alongside matching policy positions; a Liberal Democrat spokesperson separately called for transparency on the unclear answers.

The evidentiary core, the loan-to-donation conversion and the same-day transfer, donation and repayment sequence, sits on Reform UK's own filed accounts, Electoral Commission records and Companies House filings for Britain Means Business, all independently checkable; the Financial Times is cited as having separately reported the conversion was "partly financed" by Cottrell, giving the core transaction two-outlet corroboration. The £575,000 gap in Cottrell's transfer and the unidentified full source behind the Robinson loan are presented as open questions rather than resolved findings, and Reform UK's non-response to the spending questions is reported as such rather than filled in with speculation.
