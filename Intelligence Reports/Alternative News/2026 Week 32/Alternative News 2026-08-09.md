## Story 1: The Blair-era loophole behind Palantir's small UK tax bill

**Source:** openDemocracy | https://www.opendemocracy.net/the-big-blair-era-loophole-behind-palantirs-tiny-tax-bill/
**Author:** Jade-Ruyu Yan | **Published:** 7 August 2026, 2:23pm (updated 8 August 2026) | **Read time:** ~7 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Per the granularity rule this is a catch-up: it carries a 7 August date, updated 8 August, and follows on from openDemocracy's own 26 June exclusive on the same company that the 5 August report did not catch.

openDemocracy traces the mechanism behind Palantir's low UK corporation tax bill to a specific provision passed in a May 2003 Finance Bill under Tony Blair's government, which lets companies claim tax relief on the gap between an employee share's market value and its original purchase price when granted as compensation. The piece cites the Treasury's own contemporaneous estimate that the measure would cost around £95 million in forgone tax by 2007-08, and reports that by 2022 Palantir alone had generated a tax deduction of around £230 million using the identical mechanism, a figure the company has not disputed. In 2024 Palantir's effective UK tax rate was around 8 percent against a standard rate of 25 percent.

Palantir's UK and European CEO, Louis Mosley, is quoted defending the scheme as "a generous share scheme for our UK employees" that results in "more tax paid by Palantir to the Treasury, not less," pointing to $148 million the company says it has paid in total UK taxes, and arguing that employees' income tax and National Insurance liabilities offset the corporate deduction as Parliament intended. Dan Neidle of Tax Policy Associates broadly agrees it is not avoidance "legally or morally." Set against that, University of Mannheim tax professor Christoph Spengel calls the deduction "technically justifiable" but says the real question is "is this fair"; University of Michigan's Reuven Avi-Yonah notes the mechanism itself is not new but Palantir's AI-driven profitability now makes its scale unusual, and adds that "ultimately it's the taxpayers who fund the money that goes to Palantir... they just grab the money and don't pay any tax"; UCLA's Kimberly Clausing argues the same public objectives could be met "much more easily if we had the will to use our corporation tax properly." The piece notes the UK and US are international outliers in permitting deductions this generous, contrasting with Germany, and situates the story against Palantir's more than £670 million in UK public contracts, which make Britain the company's second-largest market after the US.

The evidentiary core, the 2003 legislative provision, the Treasury's own cost estimate and Palantir's own accounts showing the scale of the deduction, sits on public record: Companies House filings, SEC filings and Hansard-level legislative history, all independently checkable. Palantir's defence is included in full rather than omitted, and the critical quotes are attributed to named academics rather than presented as the reporter's own conclusion; nobody quoted disputes the underlying figures, only whether the outcome is fair.

## Story 2: State Department moves to deny visas over alleged "far-left" ties, naming Hasan Piker and Christian Smalls

**Source:** The Intercept | https://theintercept.com/2026/08/08/state-department-rubio-leftists-visas-terrorism/
**Author:** Matt Sledge | **Published:** 8 August 2026, 6:06am ET | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Per the granularity rule this is a catch-up: it carries yesterday's date and was not yet reported when the 8 August report was written.

The Intercept reports that Secretary of State Marco Rubio has announced a policy denying US visas to foreign nationals accused of ties to "far-left terrorism," citing Trump's National Security Presidential Memorandum-7. A State Department-linked report cited in the announcement names streamer Hasan Piker, Amazon Labor Union founder Christian Smalls, the National Lawyers Guild and the Democratic Socialists of America as examples of entities it considers aligned with far-left terrorism, though none of the named individuals or groups have been charged with any offence. The policy language targets alleged support for violent activity, "economic sabotage" or logistical support to far-left groups; no visa denials under the policy have yet been announced and implementation details remain unclear.

Peter Margulies, a law professor at Roger Williams University, warned the vague "economic sabotage" language could be used against constitutionally protected speech, including by foreign students already in the US, and noted the policy sets no clear limits on how broadly it could be applied. Carrie DeCell, senior staff attorney at the Knight First Amendment Institute, is quoted saying "the vagueness is the point. It's intended to send a broad chilling effect through the public." The piece situates the move alongside past State Department visa restrictions that were successfully challenged in court, and legal experts anticipate similar challenges here once the policy is actually used to deny an application.

The evidentiary core, the policy's existence and its citation of NSPM-7 and the named-entities report, rests on the State Department's own public announcement and document; the chilling-effect and overbreadth warnings are attributed to named legal experts rather than asserted as fact by the reporter, and the piece is explicit that no denials have yet occurred, so its claims about impact are anticipatory rather than documented harm.
