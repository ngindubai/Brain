## Story 1: Owner of company spearheading Kyrgyzstan's planned "eco-city" had business links with government official's son

**Source:** OCCRP | https://www.occrp.org/en/news/owner-of-company-spearheading-kyrgyzstans-planned-eco-city-had-business-links-with-govt-officials-son
**Author:** OCCRP staff | **Published:** 29 July 2026 | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

OCCRP reports that RedStone Group, the company selected to build Kyrgyzstan's planned Asman eco-city, a $20 billion project that would hand the developer 4,015 hectares of land for 49 years, is owned by Chinese businessman Tu Yunfei. Corporate registry records show Tu previously co-owned a separate company, Zet Systems, with Nizamidin Sabirov, the son of Ravshan Sabirov, who heads Kyrgyzstan's National Investment Agency, the body overseeing the Asman project.

The reporting draws on corporate registry filings, an August 2025 draft investment agreement found in a government database, an earlier 2024 draft of the same agreement showing how the project's terms shifted over time, and a December 2025 televised interview with Elmar Zhunusov, head of the State Directorate handling the project. RedStone was incorporated in July 2024; Tu Yunfei became its sole shareholder after the draft agreement was in circulation. The company's director at the time it was named in the draft agreement, Leila Imin, previously ran Zet Systems, the same company Tu co-owned with the investment agency chief's son. Earlier drafts of the project also named Module House, a company linked to Kyrgyzstan's Presidential Administrative Directorate, headed by Kanybek Tumanbaev. President Sadyr Japarov is named as the project's political champion.

OCCRP put its findings to RedStone Group, the National Investment Agency, Imin, Nizamidin Sabirov, Tumanbaev and Module House; none responded.

The project's scale is the significance here: $20 billion is close to the whole of Kyrgyzstan's 2025 GDP, awarded to a developer whose ownership history runs directly through the family of the state official responsible for approving and overseeing the deal. That is a textbook conflict-of-interest structure, laid out through paper trail rather than allegation, in a country where a single megaproject of this size effectively sets the state's economic direction for a generation.
