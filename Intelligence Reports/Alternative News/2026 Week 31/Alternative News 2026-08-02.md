## Story 1: Sanctioned Kinahan cartel figures held live Dubai residence visas

**Source:** Bellingcat (with The Sunday Times) | https://www.bellingcat.com/news/2026/08/01/welcome-to-dubai-kinahan-cartels-visas-revealed/
**Author:** Connor Plunkett, Peter Barth, Beau Donelly and John Mooney | **Published:** 1 August 2026 | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** [[Comparison Network]]

Per the granularity rule this is a catch-up, not today's story: it carries yesterday's date and had not yet been checked when the 1 August report ran.

Bellingcat's financial investigations team, working with The Sunday Times, reports that senior figures in Ireland's Kinahan drug-trafficking organisation held active or recently renewed UAE residence permits despite a $5 million US Treasury bounty and formal sanctions naming them as a transnational criminal network. Christopher Kinahan Jr, listed as a "sales officer" at a Dubai tourism firm, had his permit renewed on 20 July 2026. Bernard Clancy's permit, tied to a wholesale goods company, was renewed in January 2026. Cartel founder Christy Kinahan's permit had lapsed and accrued a four-figure overstay fine; an associate, Ian Dixon, was found to owe more than $11,000 in fines for an 852-day overstay, implying his continued residence went unchallenged for over two years.

The method was straightforward and repeatable: the team took identifying details already published in US Treasury sanctions notices, birth dates, ID numbers, nationalities, and ran them through the UAE's own public unified-number immigration lookup portal. That portal returned visa status, employer and renewal history directly. No leak or FOI was needed; the data was sitting behind a standard government lookup tool that does not appear to cross-reference against international sanctions lists. The team corroborates its Dubai findings against UAE government fine records and older public material (a 2015 LinkedIn post placing Christy Kinahan in Turkey) to establish the pattern over time rather than a single snapshot.

A former UK National Crime Agency deputy director is quoted calling the continued presence of sanctioned figures under live UAE paperwork implausible without institutional failure somewhere in the chain; a solicitor with Dubai experience goes further, telling the reporters the pattern "shows there's been corruption involved." Neither claim is Bellingcat's own conclusion, both are attributed named-source opinion, and the piece does not produce a document or admission proving corruption specifically, as distinct from bureaucratic non-enforcement. That distinction matters and is addressed further below.

## Story 2: On-the-ground reporting undercuts Trump and New York Times claim of a Russian spy base in Cuba

**Source:** Drop Site News, republished by Belly of the Beast Cuba | https://www.bellyofthebeastcuba.com/trump-nyt-russian-spy-base-cuba-school (Drop Site's own page, https://www.dropsitenews.com/p/trump-nyt-cuba-russian-spy-base-lourdes, returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch)
**Author:** Reed Lindsay | **Published:** 30 July 2026 (also carried 31 July by a second republishing outlet) | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Per the granularity rule this is a catch-up: the story ran two to three days before this report and was missed because the 31 July and 1 August runs' Drop Site searches did not surface it.

Trump's January 2026 executive order justifying an escalated fuel blockade on Cuba alleged the island hosts "Russia's largest overseas signals intelligence facility." The New York Times followed in March, reporting the Lourdes site "bristles with antennas and eavesdropping equipment" capable of monitoring US military sites and Mar-a-Lago, and that Russia had reopened the base in 2014 after Putin closed it in 2001.

Reed Lindsay spent two days on the ground at the site named in both claims and found Cuba's University of Computer Sciences (UCI), a functioning higher-education campus founded in 2002, the year after the Russians actually left. He photographed the site and interviewed the engineer who oversaw the base-to-campus conversion, a resident art professor, and other staff and graduates. What he documents: unarmed civilian guards, open public access, two standard mobile-network antennas belonging to Cuba's state telecoms provider, and a single deteriorating Soviet-era watchtower left over from the original base. No checkpoints, no restricted zones, no signals-intelligence hardware, and university sources describe the spy-base claim as absurd on its face given daily open access to the site by students and outside visitors.

The piece names the Trump administration and New York Times reporter Michael Crowley directly as having advanced an unsupported factual claim that underwrote a foreign-policy action with a documented humanitarian cost, ongoing blackouts under the fuel blockade. The reporting is a direct site visit against a specific, checkable factual claim, closer to the strongest end of this register's evidentiary range even though it is exclusively critical of the claim's originators and offers no comment from the Times or the administration in response.
