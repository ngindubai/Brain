## Story 1: BRICS held together in New Delhi by saying less, not more, about the wars its own members are fighting

**Source:** Responsible Statecraft | https://responsiblestatecraft.org/2026-brics-summit/
**Author:** Sarang Shidore, Director, Global South Program, Quincy Institute | **Published:** 13 September 2026 | **Read time:** ~5 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

The 18th BRICS summit in New Delhi produced a joint declaration at all, which Shidore treats as the notable outcome given two of the bloc's own members, Iran and the UAE, are on opposing sides of an active shooting war. His piece is built by setting this year's New Delhi text directly against last year's Rio de Janeiro declaration, and the comparison is where the actual finding sits: language on the Middle East has been quietly downgraded from explicit condemnation of military strikes to generic "deep concern over continued escalation," and every reference to the Ukraine conflict that had appeared in BRICS declarations for years has been dropped outright. Criticism of Israel's conduct in Lebanon and Gaza survived into the new text; a new line on Cuba's economic blockade was added.

Shidore's reading is that expansion has cost BRICS the thing it used to trade on, a bloc able to state unified positions the G7 wouldn't. With Iran and the UAE both now members and actively fighting each other, and with Russia's war still live, the only way to get every capital to sign the same document was to say less about the wars several of them are actually in. He notes the summit came together despite failed foreign ministers' meetings in the preceding months, which makes the diplomatic management itself part of the story, not just the wording.

This is comparative document analysis rather than a documents leak or an accountability finding: Shidore works from the two publicly available declaration texts and the New Development Bank's own material, with no anonymous sourcing and no leaked internal record of the negotiating fights that presumably produced the softened language. What the piece does establish cleanly, because it is checkable against the primary texts themselves, is the fact of the change: specific passages present in 2025 are absent or watered down in 2026. What it can't show, because nothing here is a leaked negotiating record, is which delegation pushed for which cut.

The evidentiary core, two public declarations read side by side by a named regional specialist, is solid for what it claims: a verifiable textual comparison, not a disclosure. The interpretive layer, that the change signals a structural cost of BRICS expansion, is Shidore's own analytical framing rather than something the text differences alone prove.
