## Story 1: ICE reopens a Kansas prison a federal judge called "an absolute hell hole," hiring the same contractor whose understaffing caused the violence

**Source:** The Intercept | https://theintercept.com/2026/09/18/leavenworth-kansas-corecivic-prison-ice-staffing/
**Author:** Nathalie Levey | **Published:** 18 September 2026 | **Read time:** ~7 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

CoreCivic has reopened its former federal prison in Leavenworth, Kansas as a 1,033-bed ICE detention facility, the same building a federal judge described as "an absolute hell hole" when it closed in 2021 after years of documented violence and chronic understaffing. Levey's reporting draws on a 2017 Justice Department inspector general audit that flagged the facility's failure to staff mandatory security posts, and a joint 2021 ACLU/federal public defender count of nearly 37 violent incidents a month between May and July that year. A Leavenworth Police Department incident report details a near-fatal multi-stab-wound assault on guard Marcia Levering, who is named and interviewed alongside former guard Bill Rogers (also assaulted) and 13-year employee Shari Rich.

The piece traces how CoreCivic secured local approval to reopen: building permits dated March 2025, an email from CoreCivic vice-president John Malloy to city commissioners, and a cease-and-desist letter from the company's deputy general counsel when the city pushed back, on top of the federal-authority argument CoreCivic ultimately used to bypass local permitting altogether. CoreCivic promised $28.58-an-hour starting pay and local construction contracts; current job listings show $21.19-$28.25, and the contractors hired came from Texas, not Kansas. CoreCivic spokesperson Ryan Gustin defended the company's practices but declined to answer questions about the 2021 attack on Levering specifically.

Sourcing is solid throughout: a federal inspector general audit, an independent ACLU/public defender incident count, police records, internal company correspondence and SEC filings, plus named workers and prisoners on the record. The only anonymous-adjacent element is CoreCivic's own non-answers on the Levering assault, which the piece flags rather than papers over. This is the kind of story the register does well: a company's public safety assurances tested directly against its own documented record.

## Story 2: Robert Kraft says he has backed Palestinians "for decades." His foundation's tax filings show $3 million-plus to Friends of the IDF, AIPAC's super PAC and allied advocacy groups

**Source:** The Intercept | https://theintercept.com/2026/09/18/robert-kraft-macklemore-ed-sheeran-israel-donations/
**Author:** Jonah Valdez | **Published:** 18 September 2026 | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Valdez's review of Kraft Family Foundation and Kraft Group tax filings since 2006 sets Robert Kraft's recent public claim of supporting Palestinians "for decades" through job-creating "initiatives" against a documented donation record that runs the other way. The foundation gave $196,000 across seven donations to Friends of the IDF, earmarked for "education for young Jewish soldiers," plus $123,000 to Brothers for Life and Friends of Israel Disabled Veterans. Kraft's LLC, the Kraft Group, has given more than $3 million since 2022 to AIPAC's United Democracy Project super PAC. Further sums went to CAMERA ($63,500), the Middle East Media Research Institute ($150,000 across 2008-2018) and the Investigative Project on Terrorism ($50,000), and Kraft founded the Foundation to Combat Antisemitism with a $20 million gift in 2019.

The through-line the piece draws is that Friends of the IDF's own lone-soldier programme, which Kraft's money helps fund, has paid out more than $20 million to Jewish Americans serving in Israeli military reserves during the Gaza campaign, a direct military-support function that sits uneasily against his stated Palestinian-support framing. Both Kraft and the recipient organisations declined to comment when approached.

This is a documents story, not an allegations one: named tax filings, named dollar amounts, named recipients, set against Kraft's own public statement. There is no dispute about whether the donations happened, only about whether Kraft's framing of his own giving is honest, and the piece lets the filings answer that rather than asserting it.

## Story 3: A bipartisan drug-transparency bill loses its enforcement teeth days before a Senate vote, after $1 million in generic-drug lobbying

**Source:** ProPublica | https://www.propublica.org/article/clear-labels-act-drug-safety-senate-vote
**Author:** Naisha Roy, with Brandon Roberts | **Published:** 18 September 2026 | **Read time:** ~5 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

The Clear Labels Act, which passed committee in July with only Senator Rand Paul dissenting, has been revised before its full Senate vote to drop the requirement that drug labels carry a unique facility identifier (a DUNS number) and to stretch the compliance deadline from one year to five. ProPublica's own prior reporting found DUNS numbers let investigators trace a drug back to its actual manufacturing plant 69% of the time, against a laborious, often-inconclusive process of address verification and manual review when only a factory address is listed, the standard the weakened bill now falls back to. Vague "place of business" language could let manufacturers list a US headquarters instead of the real overseas factory, reopening the exact loophole the bill was written to close.

The Association for Accessible Medicines, the generic manufacturers' trade body, spent over $1 million on healthcare lobbying between January and July while opposing the bill on cost grounds, and lobbying-disclosure filings back that spending figure directly. Original sponsors Rick Scott and Kirsten Gillibrand praised the amended bill without addressing what was cut; retired Army Colonel Vic Suarez called it "a clear watering down of the original bill," Stanford's Dr Kevin Schulman said "five years after it passes might as well be a lifetime," and former FDA inspector Peter Baker was blunt that a one-year timeline was always achievable: "If they wanted to do it in one year, they could." The bill follows ProPublica's earlier investigation finding the FDA had approved drug imports from factories with "leaks, mold and contaminated water," tied to thousands of adverse reactions, which is the harm the original bill was meant to make traceable.

Sourcing is strong: lobbying-disclosure filings, committee vote records, and on-record criticism from named regulators, physicians and a former military health official, layered on ProPublica's own earlier documentary findings about contaminated facilities. Nothing here rests on an anonymous source.

## Story 4: Congressional testimony undercuts the Pentagon's own claims about AI targeting accuracy, as Palantir's Maven system is credited with 13,000 strikes in 38 days

**Source:** Responsible Statecraft | https://responsiblestatecraft.org/ai-palantir-weapons/
**Author:** Nick Cleveland-Stout | **Published:** 18 September 2026 | **Read time:** ~5 min
**Related vault notes:** [[Agent Org]] (AI deployment accountability, adjacent watch).

Testimony to the Tom Lantos Human Rights Commission is the spine of this piece: Carnegie's Steven Feldstein told the hearing that real-world accuracy for military AI targeting systems "may be closer to 50%" against vendor claims nearer 90%, Human Rights Watch's Deborah Brown said the evidence "doesn't suggest that this is limiting the harm," and Amnesty's Amanda Klasing argued accuracy is beside the point once "proportionality is blown out of the water" by the scale AI enables. Against that testimony, the US military's Maven Smart System (built by Palantir, under CEO Alex Karp and executive Shyam Sankar) is credited with striking 13,000 targets in 38 days during the recent conflict with Iran, including a 28 February strike on an Iranian girls' elementary school that killed more than 170 people. Israel's parallel Lavender system flagged 37,000 Palestinian men as targets, with strikes proceeding on approvals that accepted 15-20 civilian deaths per named target. The Pentagon's own Civilian Protection Center has had its staff cut from 40 to 9.

The article's argument is that AI targeting has not reduced civilian harm, it has scaled the volume of strikes faster than any accuracy gains could offset, and the institutional capacity meant to catch the resulting harm (the Civilian Protection Center) has been cut rather than expanded alongside it.

Sourcing is solid for the testimony and casualty figures, which are attributed to named expert witnesses at a public congressional hearing rather than anonymous officials. The specific accuracy percentages (50% vs claimed 90%) are Feldstein's own estimate, not an audited figure, and should be read as an expert's informed assessment rather than a verified statistic; the Lavender casualty-ratio figures are carried over from prior, already-published reporting on that system rather than newly obtained here.

## Story 5: A translated Agência Pública investigation finds falsified safety records and 70-hour weeks on Brazilian data-centre sites feeding the AI boom

**Source:** openDemocracy (original reporting by Agência Pública) | https://www.opendemocracy.net/ais-data-centre-construction-boom-is-crushing-workers/
**Author:** Naira Hofmeister | **Published:** 18 September 2026 | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** [[Agent Org]] (AI infrastructure supply chain, adjacent watch).

Agência Pública's investigation, translated and edited by openDemocracy, documents labour conditions at Scala Data Centers construction sites in Brazil (Scala is owned by Japan's SoftBank and was founded by DigitalBridge). Named workers Francisco Rodrigues, Rafael Suriano, Anderson Soares and Renato Gonçalves describe 70-hour weeks against a legal 54-hour limit, unpaid overtime, missing hazard pay, and injuries including electrical shocks and partial hearing loss. The reporters reviewed 11 labour court cases and identified three ongoing investigations by Brazil's labour prosecutor's office, plus attendance records a court noted showed suspiciously "symmetrical entries," and a work-authorisation form backdated to the date of an accident, evidence of falsified paperwork rather than isolated complaints.

The investors named behind Scala's expansion include the International Finance Corporation (a World Bank arm), the Coatue fund, Canadian pension fund IMCO, the Olayan Group, Brazil's state development bank BNDES, and major commercial banks including Banco do Brasil, Bradesco, Itaú and UBS, subcontractor Multiway is named as the entity directly responsible for the assembly work where injuries occurred. Osasco and Region Metalworkers' Union president Gilberto Almazan is quoted on the pattern of dismissals for reporting violations, and several workers spoke only on condition of anonymity citing reprisal fears, disclosed as such rather than presented as on-the-record.

Sourcing mixes solid documentary material, court cases, a labour prosecutor's ongoing inquiries, falsified records identified by a judge, with named and anonymous worker testimony handled transparently. The Britain-facing angle is a single line noting the UK's own data-centre construction boom faces worker shortages, not a parallel UK investigation, so this counts as European-outlet coverage of a global AI-infrastructure story rather than a UK-specific one.
