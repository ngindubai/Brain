## Story 1: United Airlines has been shipping F-35 parts and tank components to Israel on ordinary passenger flights

**Source:** The Intercept | https://theintercept.com/2026/09/09/united-airlines-flights-israel-weapons-hardware-transport/
**Author:** Noah Hurowitz | **Published:** 9 September 2026, 1:46pm ET | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

A joint investigation by Movement Research Unit, the Palestinian Youth Movement, People's Embargo for Palestine and the Democratic Socialists of America, reported by The Intercept, used open-source cargo manifest data to track roughly 2,761 military-linked shipments carried aboard United Airlines passenger aircraft between July 2025 and February 2026. The flights ran mainly on routes 84 and 90, Newark to Tel Aviv, meaning ordinary paying passengers were flying alongside war materiel in the belly of the same aircraft. Researchers sampled 546 of the shipments in detail and identified 28 tank-component consignments, 131 shipments of F-35 fighter jet parts, 67 other aircraft components and more than 100 communications and display-system shipments.

Most of the traced cargo, 2,070 shipments, went to facilities belonging to Elbit Systems, Israel's largest domestically-owned defence contractor; a further 78 went to Lockheed Martin's Israeli headquarters, with additional consignments reaching Israeli military installations directly. The investigation also mapped a domestic feeder network: 235 of the shipments arrived at Newark on earlier domestic United flights from Dallas-Fort Worth, Houston, Atlanta and Los Angeles, meaning the supply chain runs through the ordinary US passenger network well before it reaches the transatlantic leg.

The Intercept notes it withheld some of the researchers' methodology details at their request, to avoid interference with the ongoing tracking effort, so the published piece is a summary of primary data United declined to comment on. A Democratic Socialists of America representative frames the throughline plainly: "United, consistently and on a regular basis, ships military materiel that is used by the Israeli military" in operations in Gaza and Lebanon.

The evidentiary core, open-source cargo manifests aggregated and sampled by named advocacy-researcher groups rather than a leaked internal document, is solid but activist-sourced: strong on the "what" (specific shipment counts, routes, recipients) and weaker on the "why now" or any internal United decision-making, since nothing here comes from inside the airline itself. Worth reading as a mapping exercise rather than a whistleblower story.

## Story 2: Police departments are using Flock's surveillance-camera network to investigate people for "loitering"

**Source:** The Intercept | https://theintercept.com/2026/09/09/flock-cameras-police-loitering-curfew-surveillance/
**Author:** Sam Biddle | **Published:** 9 September 2026, 6:00am ET | **Read time:** ~7 min
**Related vault notes:** [[Agent Org]]

Records obtained from Plano, Illinois show local police ran more than 1,200 searches of Flock Safety's camera network, which spans over 120,000 cameras nationwide, under the category "Curfew/Loitering/Vagrancy Violations." The searches were not limited to local departments: requests came in from across Illinois, from neighbouring states, and from as far away as Georgia, where Norcross police alone queried 6,098 camera networks on that same justification.

The examples The Intercept lists are strikingly minor. A Tennessee Bureau of Investigation search targeted people simply present at "a planned protest site." West Palm Beach police searched for a person who had been "dropped off" and was suspected of being a vagrant. In LaGrange, Georgia, officers ran a search across more than 6,000 camera networks because someone had "lingered at a stop sign around midnight." Many of the search records carried no case number attached, which the piece reads as a sign of casual, unaccountable use rather than tightly scoped investigative work.

Jay Stanley of the ACLU places this inside a familiar pattern: "This is in line with what we see with these technologies sold on the basis of movie plot scenario," while what actually happens is "over-policing" of ordinary, mundane behaviour, with oversight left entirely to Flock and the police departments using it, not any outside body. The Intercept adds the historical spine to the story: loitering and vagrancy laws trace to the Jim Crow era, built explicitly as pretextual tools against Black Americans, and the Supreme Court struck down similarly vague ordinances as unconstitutional in 1972, a precedent this generation of camera-driven enforcement appears to route around rather than confront.

The evidentiary core, public records obtained from named departments showing actual search categories and counts, is solid and documentary. What the story does not establish is outcome data, how many of these loitering-flagged searches led to any charge, stop or arrest, so the piece demonstrates the scale and casualness of the practice convincingly without yet showing its downstream harm in the same records.

## Story 3: A leaked six-month paper trail shows how Israeli universities and their lawyers worked to break European academic boycotts from the inside

**Source:** Investigate Europe (with Reporters United) | https://www.investigate-europe.eu/posts/revealed-european-universities-struggle-to-cut-out-israeli-research-partners and https://www.investigate-europe.eu/posts/revealed-inside-the-israeli-campaign-to-derail-european-university-boycotts
**Authors:** Maria Maggiore, Konstantina Maltepioti, Ana Lázaro, Ludovica Jona, Leïla Miñano, Erell Renaudeau | **Published:** 9 September 2026 | **Read time:** ~9 min combined
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

A six-month cross-border investigation, published jointly with EUobserver, Follow the Money, Il Fatto Quotidiano, Knack, Le Monde, Le Vif and Publico.es, traces how dozens of European universities that pledged in 2025 to suspend or end ties with Israeli institutions over the Gaza war have mostly stayed inside EU-funded research programmes regardless. The investigation's second part identifies the machinery behind that gap: the Association of Israeli University Heads (VERA), which disclosed spending $800,000 over two years on legal work defending Israeli academic participation in European research, and Kesher, a Brussels-based law firm VERA engaged, which worked with 27 universities across nine European countries using legal letters, local-firm partnerships and direct pressure on national authorities.

The documentary basis is unusually strong for this register: a 14-page Kesher letter from November 2024 to the European Commission defending Tel Aviv University and the Weizmann Institute against a Ghent University exclusion attempt; a "VERA State of Affairs" spreadsheet logging boycott incidents and interventions; a January 2025 Kesher interim report describing "coordinated attacks from different universities"; and internal Israeli government meeting minutes detailing a Council for Higher Education plan to fund a dedicated anti-boycott office with €1.7 million. At La Sorbonne, the leaked documents show Kesher tracking, and apparently working to derail, a student motion calling for severed Israeli ties, citing "close collaboration" with Israel's ambassador Joshua Zarka and unnamed French political figures; both the embassy and the university deny this characterisation. In Spain, ACOM, a separate pro-Israel lobbying group, has filed lawsuits against eight universities including Granada over their decisions to cut ties, with the Granada case still before the Supreme Court.

The European Commission's own position, stated by Research Commissioner Iliana Ivanova in a July 2024 letter, is that "termination solely on the basis of nationality would be improper and would amount to discrimination." No Israeli institution has been suspended from a Horizon Europe grant on ethical grounds under Article 14 since the war began, a contrast the investigation draws sharply against the Commission's 2022 decision to suspend 29 Russian research projects within ten days of the invasion of Ukraine, cited by Free University of Brussels professor Yiorgos Vassalos as the double standard at the heart of the story. Separately, an EUobserver investigation folded into this piece found the Commission's own legal service advised as far back as 2017 that cooperation with Israel could be suspended without a formal vote, advice that has never been acted on.

The evidentiary core, leaked internal correspondence, spreadsheets and government meeting minutes corroborated across seven co-publishing outlets, is as strong as documents-based journalism gets in this register. The open question the piece leaves unresolved is legal rather than factual: whether Kesher's lobbying constitutes ordinary legal advocacy or improper interference in university governance is a characterisation dispute the story lays out the facts for without adjudicating itself.

## Story 4: Ectopic pregnancy deaths have nearly doubled since 2020, and the rise is sharpest in states with the strictest abortion bans

**Source:** ProPublica | https://www.propublica.org/article/ectopic-pregnancy-deaths-doubled-analysis
**Authors:** Andrea Suozzo, Agnel Philip | **Published:** 9 September 2026, 6:00am ET | **Read time:** ~7 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

ProPublica's analysis of CDC data, part of its ongoing "Life of the Mother" series, finds nearly 200 women died from ectopic pregnancy between 2020 and 2025, against roughly 100 in the prior six-year period, close to a doubling nationally. The rise is not even: in states with bans starting at six weeks or earlier, the death rate reached 13.1 per million live births by 2023-2025, against 7.1 per million in states without bans and 9.8 nationally. Ectopic pregnancies, where the embryo implants outside the uterus and cannot be carried to term, require termination to prevent the fallopian tube rupturing; almost every state ban carries a written exception for them, but the data suggests the exception is not translating into timely treatment.

The mechanism the reporting identifies is diagnostic delay under legal pressure: early ultrasounds frequently cannot confirm where a pregnancy has implanted, and doctors, wary of prosecution if they terminate a pregnancy that later proves viable, wait for definitive confirmation before acting. One doctor told a patient that intervening too early could mean "10 years in the poky," a line ProPublica uses to illustrate the chilling effect directly rather than infer it. Two named cases carry the story: Kyleigh Thurman in Texas, whose fallopian tube ruptured after days of delay, with regulators later finding her hospital failed proper screening and violated federal emergency-care law; and Leitaea Lowrimore, turned away by three hospitals across Oklahoma and Arkansas before finally receiving treatment in Kansas, who is now suing under the same federal emergency-stabilisation statute.

ProPublica also reports a data and monitoring gap on the federal side: the CDC's Division of Reproductive Health has lost most of its roughly 100 staff, and the Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System team is on administrative leave, meaning the agency holding the underlying data is simultaneously being hollowed out. Dr Alice Abernathy, an OB-GYN quoted in the piece, states the standard the reporting is measuring against: "A death related to ectopic pregnancy should really be a never event."

The evidentiary core, CDC mortality data analysed directly by ProPublica's own reporters, is as strong as this register gets, an original statistical finding rather than a repackaged press release. The causal claim, that legal fear rather than some other factor drives the excess deaths in ban states, rests on the two detailed case studies and named doctors' accounts of hesitation rather than on hospital-level data proving intent in every death counted, which is the honest limit of what mortality statistics alone can show.

## Story 5: A federal audit finds the Postal Service mishandled ballots in this year's primaries, ahead of a Trump order to police mail voting itself

**Source:** ProPublica | https://www.propublica.org/article/postal-service-audit-primary-election-ballots
**Author:** Jen Fifield | **Published:** 9 September 2026, 8:35pm EDT | **Read time:** ~5 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

A 4 September 2026 audit by the USPS Office of Inspector General, covering 14 processing facilities and 73 delivery units across eight states during the 2026 primaries, found roughly 30% of delivery units checked (22 of 73) had no election-mail training materials posted, about 12% of facilities (10 of 82) had management unfamiliar with correct postmarking procedure, and 13 of 82 facilities failed to follow dispatch procedures meant to ensure ballots leave the same day. The audit's sharpest single finding is Pennsylvania-specific: 56 ballots were postmarked with the wrong date, and one facility held 108 ballots until election morning rather than dispatching them promptly. Overall on-time performance was still high, nearly 99% of ballots reached election officials on schedule, up from 98% in 2024, so the audit describes a system with a good aggregate record and a persistent minority of facility-level failures underneath it.

The context ProPublica adds is what turns a routine inspector-general report into a live story: a Trump administration executive order now requires USPS to help enforce mail-in voting rules using voter-eligibility lists, a system that has not been tested and would run through exactly the facilities and training gaps this audit documents. David Becker of the Center for Election Innovation and Research puts the stakes in one line: USPS has "a long way to go to meet its core obligations" on timely election mail, a judgement made before the new enforcement role is even operational. USPS's own response is that "overall ballot mail performance is strong," a defensible reading of the aggregate figures that does not address the facility-level training and postmarking gaps the audit itself found.

The evidentiary core, a federal Inspector General audit with facility-level findings, is as solid as government-oversight sourcing gets; nothing here rests on anonymous sourcing or reconstruction. The forward-looking risk, that these gaps will matter more once USPS is enforcing eligibility rules rather than just delivering mail, is the reporting's own inference from the timing, reasonable but not yet demonstrated by any incident.

## Story 6: The Trump administration says it has stopped funding Ukraine. The real bill is $25-40 billion a year, mostly hidden in classified budgets

**Source:** Responsible Statecraft | https://responsiblestatecraft.org/ukraine-weapons-aid/
**Author:** Jennifer Kavanagh | **Published:** 9 September 2026 | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Jennifer Kavanagh, of the restraint-oriented think tank Defense Priorities, argues the Trump administration's claim to have ended US spending on Ukraine holds only for the two most visible mechanisms, Presidential Drawdown Authority and new Ukraine Security Assistance Initiative funding, both of which have indeed stopped. Underneath that, she totals roughly $20 billion in previously signed contracts still running through 2028, plus new congressional appropriations and State Department military financing worth about $5 billion a year in weapons transfers, none of which required a new administration decision to continue.

The larger and less visible cost, in Kavanagh's accounting, is direct operational support that never shows up as a weapons line item: intelligence sharing, logistics coordination and targeting assistance, which she estimates at $10-20 billion a year once personnel costs and classified intelligence budgets are factored in. That puts the realistic total closer to $25-40 billion annually, built from NDAA and appropriations documents, USAID Inspector General quarterly reports and Congressional Research Service intelligence-budget analysis, rather than from any single leaked figure. Kavanagh is explicit about the limits of her own method, noting the classified components are estimated ranges rather than confirmed figures, a caveat the piece states rather than glosses over.

The evidentiary core, a bottom-up reconstruction from public budget and appropriations documents plus CRS analysis, is solid for a think-tank piece; it is closer to documents-based analysis than to original investigative reporting, since nothing here is newly disclosed, and Kavanagh's own institutional position, an advocate of US restraint abroad, is worth reading against the framing choice to headline the gap between claimed and actual spending.
