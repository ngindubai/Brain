## Story 1: FOIA documents show OpenAI, Google, Anthropic and xAI built military AI hand-in-hand with the Pentagon, and the Pentagon wanted a model that rarely says no

**Source:** The Intercept | https://theintercept.com/2026/09/08/military-ai-weapons-contracts-openai-anthropic-google/ and https://theintercept.com/2026/09/08/pentagon-openai-military-contract/
**Author:** Sam Biddle | **Published:** 8 September 2026, 5:00am and 6:00am ET | **Read time:** ~10 min combined
**Related vault notes:** [[Agent Org]]

Through a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit brought with Legal Advocates for Safe Science and Technology, The Intercept obtained more than 400 pages of documents covering Pentagon contracts with OpenAI, Google, Anthropic and xAI, each worth up to $200 million and signed in July 2025. The contracts go well beyond a vendor relationship: engineers from the AI labs run tabletop exercises and "responsible AI" seminars with military personnel, the companies brief the Pentagon on frontier AI strategy while receiving briefings on military operations and threats in return, and OpenAI agreed to embed its own engineers within military units in what the documents describe as "warfighting support settings". The labs also produce their own "risk forecasting and threat ideation exercises" assessing the dangers of their own products, a self-assessment arrangement Cambridge researcher Sophia Goodfriend says is the first time the extent of this collaboration has been "spelled out in explicit terms": engineers, in her words, working "in lockstep" on systems built for surveillance, targeting and killing.

A second document, contract amendment "P00003" signed 6 February, contains language the Pentagon wanted attached to OpenAI's model: "minimal refusal rates" against military commands. Heidy Khlaaf, chief scientist at the AI Now Institute and a former OpenAI safety engineer, reads that phrase as shorthand for "little or no safeguards on the model being used." OpenAI spokesperson Nate Evans disputes the characterisation directly: "OpenAI has never agreed to contract language requiring 'minimal refusal rates.'" The Intercept's reporting rests on the document itself, so this is a live factual dispute between what the paperwork says and what the company says it agreed to, not a resolved question.

The wider context matters. Anthropic's public break with the Pentagon, and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's move to ban the company before a federal judge reversed the decision, played out visibly and was widely read as a principled stand over the ethics of military AI use. This reporting suggests the underlying contract dispute, not the ethics, was the real fault line, while Anthropic continued operating under the same category of high-value Pentagon deal as its rivals throughout. OpenAI's own public position against military use, which held until 2024, and Google's earlier pledge against weapons applications, reversed in 2025, are both now superseded by contracts embedding company staff directly into the units building targeting and warfighting tools.

Khlaaf's second concern is structural rather than about any single clause: labs assessing the risk of their own weapons-adjacent products, she argues, produce "self-beneficial skewed risks" almost by design, since the same company that stands to lose the contract is the one grading its own homework. Vivian Dong of Legal Advocates for Safe Science and Technology, the group that brought the FOIA suit, frames the disclosure plainly: "the public deserves insight" into what constraints, if any, actually bind these deployments. The documents show extensive redaction throughout, including on the specific deployment contract for OpenAI's classified network, so this reporting establishes the shape of the relationship rather than every operational detail inside it.

The evidentiary core, litigated FOIA documents plus on-record named sources including a former OpenAI safety engineer now at an independent research institute, is as strong as this register's sourcing gets. The one open thread is the direct contradiction between OpenAI's denial and the "minimal refusal rates" language The Intercept says the contract amendment contains; neither side has produced the underlying document publicly for independent inspection at time of writing.

## Story 2: The FAA is investigating an activist drone pilot who filmed cattle-ranch abuse, while the industry lobbies for rules that would ground him

**Source:** The Intercept | https://theintercept.com/2026/09/08/faa-drone-pilot-animal-abuse-cattle-farm/
**Author:** Matt Sledge | **Published:** 8 September 2026, 11:16am ET | **Read time:** ~5 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

The FAA has opened its first-ever investigation of a Direct Action Everywhere (DAE) drone pilot, Joseph Allman, over a 23 February flight above Grimmius Cattle Co.'s East Ranch in Strathmore, California, apparently triggered by a complaint the ranch filed the following day. A security worker who confronted Allman during the flight accused him of flying beyond his visual line of sight, an FAA violation Allman denies. The footage Allman's flight and others like it produced showed ranch workers kicking calves and using pliers on them; one worker was fired as a result. Grimmius has separately pushed the FAA for stricter drone rules, with chief operating officer Austin Flores framing the flyovers as a "food security vulnerability." Allman also faces separate, unrelated criminal charges over removing baby goats from a dairy farm in 2025.

DAE spokesperson Cassie King puts the stakes plainly: "We know the industry lies about us... What would be most concerning is if the FAA believes those lies." Law professor Douglas Marshall calls this kind of investigation routine, "all of 15 minutes" of agency work, which cuts against reading too much into the fact of an investigation on its own; what makes the story worth carrying is the pattern rather than the single case, an industry group with a documented abuse problem pushing the regulator toward rules that would foreclose the method that caught it.

The evidentiary core, named participants on both sides and a documented prior firing tied to the footage, is solid but this is a single-incident story, not a documents-based investigation; the significance is in the regulatory trend it illustrates rather than in any one finding.

## Story 3: Georgia has spent $1.1 million on lethal injection logistics since one execution, and a secrecy law is the only reason anyone found out

**Source:** ProPublica | https://www.propublica.org/article/georgia-lethal-injections-costs-secrecy
**Author:** Max Blau | **Published:** 8 September 2026, 5:00am | **Read time:** ~7 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Reporter Max Blau filed open-records requests after trying to identify the successor to a doctor who had overseen Georgia's execution drugs; when the Department of Corrections refused, citing the state's Lethal Injection Secrecy Act, Blau sued, and a three-judge panel sided with him. The documents that emerged, handwritten and heavily redacted, show Georgia has paid more than $1.1 million to lethal-injection contractors since the pandemic despite carrying out only one execution in that period, with annual spending on the process averaging over $150,000 across the past decade, well above pre-2017 rates. The records lack the purchase-order detail state financial policy normally requires, and the state also covers contractors' legal fees in "any non-judicial proceeding," a further layer of shielding beyond simple anonymity.

Death-penalty scholar Deborah Denno of Fordham reads the spending itself as evidence: "You wouldn't have to pay this much money if the lethal injection process was acceptable." A state attorney, quoted from a 2013 hearing, made the opposing case at the time the secrecy law was defended: "Once that compounding pharmacy's identity is revealed, how will the Department of Corrections ever get another compounding pharmacy to sell to us?" The rising cost trend, set against a shrinking pool of participating medical professionals, points to the same conclusion other states' reporting has reached: secrecy laws are increasingly a response to recruitment difficulty rather than a neutral administrative choice.

The evidentiary core, litigated public records obtained after a successful lawsuit, is documentary and about as strong as this kind of story gets; the interpretive leap, that high cost signals difficulty finding willing participants rather than some other driver, is the reporting's inference from the pattern rather than a document that states it directly.

## Story 4: Rupert Lowe's Restore Britain sells "direct democracy". Its own founding paperwork gives him sole and irreversible control

**Source:** Byline Times | https://bylinetimes.com/2026/09/08/the-undemocratic-power-grab-at-the-heart-of-restore-britain/
**Author:** Josiah Mortimer | **Published:** 8 September 2026 | **Read time:** ~6 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Restore Britain, the far-right party former Reform UK MP Rupert Lowe founded in March 2026 (polling around 3% nationally, close to 9% in the Greater Manchester mayoral race, campaigning on mass deportations, capital punishment and a burqa ban), files two incompatible sets of founding documents with two different regulators. The Electoral Commission holds Articles of Association describing the company as "limited by guarantee"; Companies House holds a version, dated five weeks later, describing it as "limited by shares", with Lowe as sole shareholder holding the single £1 share and registered as the party's Person with Significant Control on all three available grounds: 75%-plus of shares, 75%-plus of voting rights, and sole power to appoint or remove directors. The two structures cannot legally coexist under the Companies Act 2006.

The practical effect, laid out in the party's own constitution, is that Lowe appoints the National Board, which functions as the company's board of directors; there is no mechanism to remove him as leader; leadership elections are open only to sitting Restore Britain MPs, of whom there is currently one; the Board rather than the membership controls candidate selection; and annual general meetings pass no motions, receive no accounts and hold no elections. Members can vote for a leader when exactly one candidate is eligible, and the Board can revoke membership under rules it writes itself.

The evidentiary core, the party's own Companies House and Electoral Commission filings, read against each other, is documentary and independently checkable; this is a case where the finding is what the documents themselves show rather than an inference layered on top of them. The story does not resolve which set of documents governs in practice, or what regulatory consequence the contradiction might carry, leaving open whether this becomes an Electoral Commission compliance matter or simply sits as an unresolved filing discrepancy.

## Story 5: The man who wrote the UK's AI strategy just joined Anthropic, one of the companies it was meant to hold at arm's length

**Source:** openDemocracy | https://www.opendemocracy.net/matt-clifford-joining-anthropic-makes-a-joke-of-the-uks-sovereign-ai-strategy/
**Author:** Tom Darling | **Published:** 8 September 2026, 8:05am | **Read time:** ~5 min
**Related vault notes:** [[Agent Org]]

Matt Clifford, who authored the UK government's "AI Opportunities Action Plan" in 2024 (adopted in full, all fifty recommendations, in January 2025) and chaired ARIA, the agency distributing £800 million in taxpayer AI and science funding, has joined Anthropic as managing director of international affairs, running the company's government relationships outside North America. Opendemocracy's Tom Darling, of the AI-transition campaign group The New Contract, argues the sequencing is the story: Clifford initially planned to keep chairing ARIA alongside the new Anthropic role, offering only a narrow recusal from Anthropic-specific matters, before backlash forced him to step down, though he stays on as interim chair for a further six months with continued access to confidential business information about the UK firms ARIA funds. A separate Democracy for Sale investigation, cited in the piece, found one-eighth of ARIA's research funding has gone to US tech firms already; Anthropic itself made at least four acquisitions in 2026, including a $400 million purchase of Coefficient Bio, giving the company a direct commercial interest in exactly the kind of UK-to-US IP flow Clifford was nominally positioned to guard against.

Darling's proposed fixes, statutory cooling-off periods before policy officials can join the companies they regulated, public funding conditioned on UK ownership and IP retention, and government power to block strategic acquisitions, are the piece's own policy argument rather than a reported fact, and are presented as such.

The evidentiary core, Clifford's own confirmed job history and ARIA's funding-recipient data, is solid and on the record; the argument that this constitutes a meaningful conflict of interest, rather than a normal industry move, rests on the reader accepting that ARIA's remit and Anthropic's commercial interests genuinely diverge, which the piece asserts more than it proves with an internal document showing intent.

## Story 6: A Philippine ex-House Speaker and the president's cousin faces arrest over an alleged $118 million flood-control plunder scheme

**Source:** OCCRP | https://www.occrp.org/en/news/philippine-ex-speaker-arrested-in-118m-plunder-case
**Author:** Alena Koroleva | **Published:** 8 September 2026 | **Read time:** ~4 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

The Philippines' Office of the Ombudsman found probable cause to charge former House Speaker Martin Romualdez, President Ferdinand Marcos Jr.'s first cousin, along with three co-defendants including a former lawmaker and Romualdez's personal assistant, over allegedly accumulating billions of pesos in commissions, kickbacks and bribes from contractors bidding on flood-control infrastructure work between 2022 and 2025. The Sandiganbayan, the country's anti-graft court, issued arrest warrants for all four with no bail recommended. Romualdez's lawyer says the case will be tested on evidence: "We respect the courts and the legal process," with liability "determined by evidence presented in court." The presidential palace's response, that accountability applies regardless of political connection, is a notable position given the family relationship between the president and the accused.

The evidentiary core rests on the Ombudsman's own probable-cause finding and the court's arrest warrants, official state action rather than journalistic reconstruction; OCCRP's role here is compiling and contextualising a live prosecution rather than breaking original documents, so treat this as solid but institutionally-sourced rather than independently investigated.

## Story 7: A former Swiss Bankers Association chief is convicted over $101 million in payments to a Kuwaiti official's private accounts

**Source:** OCCRP | https://www.occrp.org/en/news/former-swiss-banker-handed-suspended-two-year-sentence-for-corruption
**Author:** Maryam Shenawy | **Published:** 8 September 2026 | **Read time:** ~4 min
**Related vault notes:** none directly relevant.

Switzerland's Federal Criminal Court convicted Pierre Mirabaud, 78, former partner at Geneva private bank Mirabaud & Cie and Swiss Bankers Association chair from 2003 to 2009, of foreign bribery and money laundering, over nearly 300 payments totalling 82.3 million Swiss francs ($101.7 million) made to Fahad Al-Rajaan, then director general of Kuwait's Public Institution for Social Security, between 2000 and 2012. In return, Al-Rajaan is alleged to have invested $595.2 million of the pension fund's money with Mirabaud's bank. Mirabaud pleaded guilty and cooperated throughout: "I am here to take responsibility; that's why I accepted this procedure." He received a two-year suspended sentence.

The evidentiary core, a guilty plea and court conviction following a case opened in 2012, is as solid as sourcing gets; the story is a closed legal matter rather than an ongoing investigative thread, and the modest sentence relative to the sums involved, a suspended term against $101.7 million in payments, is worth noting as a pattern this desk has seen before in financial-crime sentencing.
